01

The concept

Hyperscale data centers reject enormous amounts of heat, and much of today's cooling depends on evaporating large volumes of water through cooling towers — a real constraint as facilities are increasingly sited in drought-prone regions.

This concept borrows a principle from something much smaller: a CPU tower cooler, or a motorcycle engine's finned cylinder. Both expose their cooling geometry as their visible form, using extended surface area to move heat into moving air. Scaled to building size, a data center's exterior could do the same job — with curved fins shaped to work with prevailing wind, and small turbines placed in select channels to recover a fraction of that airflow as electricity for auxiliary loads like fans and pumps.

Cooling

Extended exterior surface area rejects more heat per degree of temperature difference, passively, in still air or moving — no water evaporated.

Airflow

Curved fin geometry channels prevailing wind across the heat-rejecting surface, reducing dependence on powered fans.

Recovery

Small turbines in select channels harvest a fraction of that wind as electricity — enough to offset auxiliary loads, not compute.

Expression

The building doesn't hide its thermal architecture behind a warehouse facade. It looks like what it's doing.

03

What the model currently suggests

Take these with real skepticism — order-of-magnitude estimates, not measured results. Run thermal-model/model.py yourself to see the full reasoning.

+140% heat rejected
A finned "wing" exterior (~3× surface area) vs. an equivalent flat wall, at the same wind speed and temperature difference. Holds even in still air.
5–7.5m/s crossover
Turbine drag reduces cooling airflow. Below this wind speed, a turbine costs more (in lost cooling) than it recovers in electricity.
Smallturbine yield
Wind-harvested power is plausible for offsetting fans and pumps — not for powering compute at any meaningful scale.
04

What this needs next

  1. Real CFD — SimScale, OpenFOAM, or Ansys Fluent on an actual fin geometry, to replace the placeholder drag and loss coefficients with measured numbers.
  2. A tabletop physical prototype — flat vs. finned enclosure, same heat source, same wind — to check the model against reality at small scale.
  3. Review from people who do this for a living — data-center thermal engineers, HVAC engineers, or an architecture firm that designs hyperscale facilities.
  4. Site wind-data analysis — the crossover finding means this concept's viability depends heavily on local average wind speed, which hasn't been checked against a real candidate site yet.

This is meant to be taken further

Not pursued for profit or exclusive credit.
