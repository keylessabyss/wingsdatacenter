The building
as the heat exchanger.

A concept for building-integrated, low-water data center cooling: exterior walls shaped as giant curved fins, carrying heat away from a closed thermal loop, working with prevailing wind and natural convection — with fans available where wind falls short, and small turbines only where conditions actually justify them.

01

The concept

Hyperscale data centers reject enormous amounts of heat. Depending on facility design and climate, heat rejection can involve cooling towers, evaporative water consumption, chillers, pumps, fans, dry coolers, or combinations of these systems. Water consumption is an especially important constraint in drought-prone regions.

This concept borrows principles from something much smaller: a CPU tower cooler, heat pipe, radiator, or motorcycle engine's finned cylinder. The building envelope becomes part of the heat-rejection system — a closed thermal loop carries compute heat to large exterior wings, where extended surface area and airflow reject it to the atmosphere. Curved channels can work with prevailing wind, fans assist when natural airflow is insufficient, and turbines remain an optional secondary feature rather than a requirement.

Cooling

Extended exterior surface area increases the opportunity to reject heat to ambient air while reducing dependence on evaporative water consumption.

Airflow

Curved Wing channels can work with prevailing wind and natural convection, while fans provide assistance when ambient airflow is insufficient.

Closed loop

Compute heat stays inside a sealed thermal transport loop; outside air cools the exterior exchanger without entering the server environment.

Expression

The building doesn't hide its thermal architecture behind a warehouse facade. It looks like what it's doing.

On turbines

Turbines are a secondary, optional feature — not the core cooling concept. They create drag, which can reduce the airflow available for cooling. The current model estimates a crossover region around 5–7.5 m/s, below which a turbine may cost more in lost cooling than it recovers in electricity. A Wing without turbines may outperform a Wing with turbines at many sites, and the cooling architecture is not designed to depend on turbines succeeding.

What the model currently suggests

Take these with real skepticism — order-of-magnitude estimates, not measured results. Run thermal-model/model.py yourself to see the full reasoning.

+140% heat rejected
Modeled result for a finned Wing exterior (~3× surface area) vs. an equivalent flat wall, same conditions. Requires CFD and physical validation.
5–7.5m/s crossover
Estimated region where optional turbine obstruction may stop being a net cooling cost. Not required for the core concept to work.
Smallturbine yield
Wind-harvested power is plausible for offsetting fans and pumps — not for powering compute at any meaningful scale.
04

What this needs next

  1. Reproduce a known CFD case — complete a conventional heatsink / conjugate heat-transfer tutorial and verify the workflow before testing Wing geometry.
  2. Single-Wing CFD — compare a flat control surface with one Wing using the same heat input, material, ambient temperature, and wind conditions.
  3. Wind-speed and geometry sweeps — study fin spacing, curvature, height, channel width, wind direction, recirculation, and pressure drop.
  4. Fan-assisted airflow study — determine whether large Wing channels can reduce required mechanical airflow power.
  5. Two-phase thermal study — investigate a sealed condenser / thermosiphon architecture for transporting heat into the Wing structure.
  6. Turbine CFD — only after the cooling geometry is understood; measure the actual cooling penalty caused by airflow obstruction.
  7. A tabletop physical prototype — flat vs. finned enclosure, same heat source and airflow, to check simulation against reality.
  8. Professional and site review — thermal, HVAC, structural, and hyperscale engineers plus real temperature, humidity, wind, corrosion, and water data.
